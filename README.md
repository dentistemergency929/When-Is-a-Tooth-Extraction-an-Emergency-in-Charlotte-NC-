## When Does a Tooth Need Emergency Attention?

A dental problem may require prompt attention when pain develops suddenly or is accompanied by swelling, bleeding, trauma, or signs of infection. Common situations include a severely fractured tooth, extensive decay, a dental abscess, or a tooth that has become loose following an injury.

Some warning signs should not be ignored:

* Severe or persistent toothache
* Swelling around the tooth, gums, jaw, or face
* Pus or a bad taste coming from an infected area
* Fever combined with dental pain or swelling
* Difficulty opening the mouth
* A tooth broken below the gumline
* Uncontrolled bleeding following an injury or dental procedure
* Difficulty swallowing or breathing

Difficulty breathing or swallowing with facial or oral swelling can indicate a potentially serious infection and requires immediate medical attention.

## Why Severe Tooth Decay Can Lead to Extraction

Tooth decay begins when bacteria and acids progressively damage the tooth structure. If decay is treated early, a dentist may be able to preserve the tooth. Advanced decay, however, can destroy enough of the tooth that restoration is no longer practical.

The CDC reported in its 2024 Oral Health Surveillance Report that approximately **20.5% of dentate adults aged 20–64 had at least one untreated decayed permanent tooth**, based on nationally representative NHANES data from 2017 through March 2020.

This statistic does not mean that these teeth require extraction. It does show how common untreated decay remains among adults and why delaying dental treatment can allow a manageable problem to become more complicated.

## How a Dentist Determines Whether Extraction Is Necessary

A dentist does not decide on extraction based only on pain. The evaluation usually considers the tooth's structural condition, the extent of infection, surrounding bone and gum tissue, and whether the tooth can be predictably restored.

The examination may include:

1. A review of symptoms and dental history.
2. A visual examination of the tooth and surrounding tissues.
3. Dental X-rays when additional information is needed.
4. Evaluation for infection or an abscess.
5. Discussion of possible alternatives to extraction.

If the tooth can be saved, preserving it may be considered. If the tooth is too damaged or presents an ongoing source of infection, removal may be recommended.

## What Happens During an Emergency Extraction?

Before removing a tooth, the dentist typically numbs the treatment area with local anesthesia. The dentist then loosens the tooth and carefully removes it from its socket. A more complex procedure may be necessary if the tooth is severely broken, impacted, or difficult to access.

After removal, gauze is generally placed over the area to help control bleeding. The dentist may also provide specific instructions for protecting the developing blood clot.

The American Dental Association maintains evidence-based guidance for managing acute dental pain, including pain following tooth removal.

## What Should You Do After Tooth Removal?

Proper aftercare supports normal healing. Patients are generally advised to follow their dentist's individual instructions, particularly during the first day.

Important precautions may include:

* Keep the gauze in place for the recommended period.
* Avoid disturbing the extraction site.
* Do not smoke or use tobacco during the recommended healing period.
* Avoid strenuous activity immediately after the procedure if instructed.
* Choose appropriate foods that do not irritate the treatment area.
* Take prescribed or recommended medication exactly as directed.
* Contact the dental office if bleeding remains heavy or symptoms become worse.

A small amount of bleeding and discomfort can occur after an extraction, but persistent or worsening symptoms should be evaluated by a dental professional.

## Preventing the Need for an Emergency Extraction

Not every extraction can be prevented, particularly when a tooth has suffered significant trauma. However, routine dental care can identify cavities, cracks, gum disease, and other problems before they become urgent.

The CDC notes that oral disease can cause pain and infection and can interfere with eating, speaking, and overall well-being. Regular examinations, daily brushing with fluoride toothpaste, cleaning between teeth, and timely treatment can all contribute to better oral health.
